# The Aging Battlefield: What If We Could Stop Our Bodies Fighting Themselves?

**AletheiaGo | Go Walkabout | Research article 001 | 17 October 2026**  
**Status:** Evidence-led thought experiment. Not a medical recommendation or a claim of a new discovery.

![Conceptual illustration of marrow, immune-cell clearance, adipose tissue and gut microbes](assets/aging-battlefield-concept.png)

*Illustrative visual only: not microscopy, not measured data, and not a proven unified mechanism.*

We tend to describe ageing as things wearing out. Muscles weaken. Our bodies handle glucose less efficiently. Some immune responses become slower, yet chronic inflammation becomes more common. Brown fat seems less responsive to cold. Certain damaged cells remain where we would prefer them to be cleared.

But what if these aren't entirely separate problems?

What if ageing is partly a story of **systems that once cooperated beginning to interfere with one another**?

Imagine a city whose emergency services become stuck on permanent alert. The firefighters never fully stand down; rubbish collectors can't keep up; messages continue to report emergencies that ended yesterday. Meanwhile, the city becomes worse at dealing with a real fire. This is a metaphor, not a biological theory in itself. But research into inflammation, senescence and metabolism suggests several places where it may be surprisingly useful.

## 1. The defence system becomes noisier, but not necessarily stronger

Two concepts help explain the paradox. **Inflammaging** means persistent, low-grade inflammation associated with ageing. **Immunosenescence** describes shifts in immune-cell composition and function that may reduce protection from infection or impair appropriate responses. They can occur together. More inflammatory activity does *not* automatically mean better immune defence.

Inflammation also has an ending: the active **resolution of inflammation**. Macrophages remove dying cells by a process called **efferocytosis**, while molecules such as resolvins, lipoxins and maresins help coordinate the return to normal. Research has shown that the act of clearing dead cells can reprogramme macrophage metabolism and promote further resolution [1]. Our question isn't whether we can abolish inflammation. We need it. It is whether we can help the body **finish the job**.

## 2. Senescent cells may keep sounding the alarm

Cells can become **senescent** after certain kinds of damage or stress: they stop dividing and may release inflammatory and tissue-remodelling signals known as the **senescence-associated secretory phenotype**, or **SASP**. Senescence also has useful roles, including limiting unwanted cell division and helping coordinate some aspects of repair. It isn't simply cellular rubbish.

But persistent senescent-cell populations can contribute to tissue dysfunction. In mouse studies, inflammatory signals from senescent cells promoted the accumulation of macrophages expressing **CD38**, an enzyme involved in consuming **NAD+**, a molecule needed for many metabolic and repair processes. Researchers demonstrated a link among senescence, CD38-rich immune cells and reduced NAD+ in ageing fat and liver tissue [2, 3].

This is a striking dot to join: cells associated with one type of age-related damage may alter the energy metabolism of *other* cells around them.

## 3. Fat is not just a storage warehouse

Three broad adipose-cell programmes matter here:

- **White fat** stores and releases energy and produces important hormones. It is not useless.
- **Brown fat** contains abundant mitochondria and can generate heat, principally through the UCP1 pathway.
- **Beige fat** can develop within white-fat depots and acquire thermogenic properties when stimulated. Some beige cells can return towards a white-like state when stimulation disappears.

In a human study of 162 volunteers exposed to mild cold, detectable cold-activated brown fat was more common in younger than older participants: over 50% in people in their twenties and below 10% in those in their fifties and sixties [4]. The study demonstrates an association, not proof that declining brown fat *causes* age-related weight gain.

In a separate experiment using human fat-derived precursor cells, researchers found that cells from older donors were poorer at forming beige fat. Increasing **SIRT1** experimentally restored some of that ability, partly through the **p53/p21** senescence pathway [5]. Other work suggests that microbial inflammatory signals acting through **LPS → TLR4 → endoplasmic reticulum stress** can suppress UCP1 activation in human adipocytes grown in the laboratory [6].

So here is the first real hypothesis: **could preserving the environment around fat cells prevent them losing their ability to respond to heat-producing signals?**

## 4. The bone marrow connection is more subtle than 'red becomes useless yellow'

Red marrow makes blood cells. During normal growth and early adulthood, much of the marrow in long bones becomes more fat-rich, while important red-marrow regions remain in the pelvis, spine, ribs and other central bones. That conversion does **not** automatically produce anaemia or reduce blood oxygen. Nor is yellow marrow an inert lump of fat. Marrow adipocytes actively influence the local stem-cell environment, bone metabolism and blood-cell development [7, 8].

Ageing can nonetheless change the marrow niche: the support cells, vessels, nerves, signalling molecules and fat cells that regulate blood-forming stem cells. Researchers are investigating whether some age-related shifts favour certain myeloid immune-cell lineages over lymphoid cells [8].

That raises our second hypothesis: **might a less well-regulated marrow environment help generate immune cells that sustain inflammation, which in turn undermines metabolic tissues?**

## 5. The gut may amplify the noise

The gut microbiome helps process food and produces metabolites that influence immune and metabolic signalling. One candidate is **lipopolysaccharide (LPS)**, a component of the outer membrane of many Gram-negative bacteria. In experimental systems, LPS can activate the **TLR4** immune receptor and suppress beige-fat thermogenic responses [6].

But this is not proof that blood LPS rises steadily in every ageing human, or that the microbiome inevitably becomes depleted. Measuring very low levels of biologically active gut-derived LPS is difficult. The right research question is about **barrier function, inflammatory signalling and metabolites**, not simply 'more bacteria equals more ageing'.

Likewise **FGF21**, a metabolic signalling hormone, often rises rather than falls with ageing. One human study found higher blood FGF21 in older adults without a matching loss of measured receptor-related gene expression in subcutaneous fat [9]. More hormone does not necessarily mean more biological benefit, but reduced responsiveness cannot be assumed either.

## 6. The extraordinary CAR-T result: a single treatment in mice

Here is the finding that prompted one of our most ambitious rabbit holes.

In 2024, researchers published a mouse study using genetically modified immune cells, **senolytic CAR-T cells**, engineered to recognise **uPAR**, a protein elevated on some senescent cells. A single administration produced long-lasting reductions in certain ageing-related problems, with improvements in exercise capacity, glucose tolerance and metabolic function in different mouse models [10].

This was **not** an exercise injection for people, a proven weight-loss medicine, or a universal way to identify all senescent cells. uPAR also exists on some non-senescent cells, which creates targeting and safety challenges. Nevertheless, the study offers a powerful demonstration: **altering the immune response to dysfunctional cells can improve whole-body metabolism in animals**.

A different 2024 mouse study found that **canagliflozin**, an SGLT2 inhibitor, could indirectly enhance senescent-cell clearance through an **AICAR/AMPK → reduced PD-L1** mechanism, making cells more vulnerable to immune surveillance [11]. This is experimental evidence about canagliflozin in mice, not a demonstrated anti-ageing effect for the SGLT2 class in humans.

## 7. A second energy route: we may not need to make everything brown

A 2025 *Nature Metabolism* paper described **SANA**, a synthetic compound that promoted **creatine-dependent thermogenesis**, including pathways not requiring UCP1, in preclinical research. A short phase 1 human trial assessed safety and found exploratory changes in weight and metabolic markers [12].

The relevance is conceptual: there may be more than one way to preserve energy expenditure. But a short early trial cannot tell us whether a compound prevents metabolic ageing, and it does not validate a food-based substitute.

## 8. A possible unifying model

The connections we are exploring look like this:

**Long-term stresses and inflammatory signals**  
→ **persistent senescent-cell signalling and altered immune-cell behaviour**  
→ **changes in CD38/NAD+ metabolism, cell clearance and tissue repair**  
→ **a less supportive environment for thermogenic fat and balanced blood-cell production**  
→ **reduced metabolic flexibility, potentially feeding back into inflammation**.

**Important:** each arrow represents a proposed connection. Some have direct evidence in mice or cultured human cells. **The complete loop has not been established in ageing humans.** Ageing also involves many mechanisms outside this model: DNA damage, epigenetic change, protein quality control, vascular health, sex hormones, muscle loss, disease and environment.

## 9. The more interesting question: maintenance rather than rejuvenation

An emerging direction is not 'How do we make every old cell young?' It is:

**What fails first in the maintenance network, and could preserving that function stop other systems deteriorating?**

For example, what if we could preserve the ability of macrophages to clear dead cells? Maintain balanced marrow stem-cell support? Keep chronic inflammatory signalling from suppressing beige-fat recruitment? Protect NAD+-dependent processes without interfering with healthy immune defence? Or help thermogenic cells retain the capacity to activate when called upon?

We don't yet know whether a combination of these ideas can materially slow human ageing. The challenge is to test them rigorously while avoiding the tempting but mistaken assumption that everything linked on a map is connected by cause and effect.
