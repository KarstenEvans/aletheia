# Make a Will. Leave a Map. Build a Legacy.

*An Aletheia guide for ordinary families in England and Wales. 18 November 2026. Version 0.1 · Published Aletheia education article, 18 November 2026 · not personal legal, tax or investment advice.*

Most families don't need a Rockefeller fortune to make useful plans. They need four clear decisions. Who receives what? Where can the paperwork be found? What should the executor do first? And could a small part of the family's wealth keep working for future generations instead of disappearing in a single windfall?

## 1. Make a will: choose people, not paperwork

A will is about choices: the people or charities who inherit, the executor who administers the estate, and what happens if a beneficiary dies first. For a typical family with a mortgage-free home, savings and two adult children, the first conversation can be quite short. A married couple needs **individual wills**, even if they make similar arrangements. Jointly owned property can also pass outside the will, depending on whether the owners are joint tenants or tenants in common.

A DIY will may be possible, but incorrect witnessing can cause real problems. In England and Wales, a valid ordinary will must be written and properly signed, generally before **two adult witnesses present together**, who then sign in the will-maker's presence. Gifts to beneficiaries who witness a will, or their spouse/civil partner, can fail. The exact drafting needs professional checking when the family or the property arrangement is complicated.

Free starting point: [GOV.UK — Make a will](https://www.gov.uk/make-will). Charities including [Will Aid](https://www.willaid.org.uk/) and [Free Wills Month](https://freewillsmonth.org.uk/) sometimes offer access to basic solicitor-written wills, subject to eligibility and appointment availability.

## 2. Family handover: give your executor a map, not a password

A will says **who gets the estate**. A family handover folder says **what exists and where to find it**. These should be different documents because the original will is submitted for probate and ordinarily becomes a public record.

A useful handover folder names the bank and ISA providers, pension schemes, insurers, property title records, major household bills, subscriptions and the location of the signed original will. It tells a trusted person where to find information and which companies must be contacted. It must **not** carry passwords, PINs, recovery codes or complete account numbers; any password-manager emergency access arrangements should be securely held elsewhere.

Banks commonly freeze accounts held solely in the deceased person's name after notification. That can stop direct debits. A qualifying joint account generally remains available to the survivor, but ownership and estate implications still need checking. The executor should know which household payments need attention. The government's [Tell Us Once](https://www.gov.uk/after-a-death/organisations-you-need-to-contact-and-tell-us-once) service does not notify all private banks, pension providers and utilities.

## 3. DIY probate: know the next step

Probate is the legal authority to administer certain assets of someone who has died. It is **not always needed**; joint tenancy and asset-provider rules can affect the answer. A named executor can often apply personally rather than employ a solicitor for the whole administration.

The ordinary sequence: register the death when authorised, locate the original will, list assets and debts, value the estate, check inheritance tax reporting and payment obligations, find out whether a grant is needed, apply through GOV.UK where required, then pay the debts and distribute what remains. Keep records. Contact a professional if the estate is disputed, insolvent, held in complex trusts or affected by foreign assets.

Start with [GOV.UK — Applying for probate](https://www.gov.uk/applying-for-probate) and the free [Citizens Advice guide](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/family/death-and-wills/dealing-with-the-financial-affairs-of-someone-who-has-died/).

## 4. Build the family legacy: make capital useful twice

Imagine Mum and Dad have built up £150,000 alongside a paid-off home. A conventional inheritance might split their savings among adult children. That may be exactly right. But siblings may also agree to retain **some** capital to help their own children with education, business opportunities, and eventually first-home deposits. A family legacy is about a shared purpose and continuing accountability, not creating heirs who never need to work.

Three broad choices deserve comparison: give inheritance outright; use a **discretionary family trust**, where trustees manage assets for defined beneficiaries; or set up a **family investment company**, where members own shares and manage agreed company investments. Trustees and directors have duties. Trusts can face inheritance-tax charges on creation, every ten years and on exits, plus investment and accountancy costs; companies also pay tax and filing costs. Neither is a magic IHT shelter. A family council, annual accounts, investment rules and a fair grants/loans policy can be started before any formal structure is chosen.

For a young first-home buyer, the most immediate opportunity might be much simpler. A parent or grandparent can **gift cash to an eligible adult child** to contribute to their Lifetime ISA. In 2026/27, someone who opened their LISA at 18–39 can generally contribute up to £4,000 per year until age 50 and earn a 25% government bonus, subject to first-home conditions. The **new First Time Buyer ISA** announced in the 2026 Budget is planned for April 2029 with no upper age restriction. It is not yet open. A Junior ISA may help younger grandchildren save, but its money belongs to the child and becomes available at 18.

A repayable family mortgage-deposit loan is **not a gift** and must be declared honestly to the mortgage lender. A family pool should not promise returns, shelter assets from care assessments or lend money on unclear terms. Start with an agreed purpose, then check the legal and tax costs before paying for structures.

## Books and music can start a conversation

Government guidance remains the first and free route. A paid printed kit such as Lawpack's standard will form, or a Ryman legal form pack, can help some uncomplicated estates, but the kit is not personalised legal advice. Optional books about family finances and the responsibilities of an executor can be useful, provided their legal and tax claims are checked against current GOV.UK guidance.

Music is an invitation to talk while relatives can still join in: [Mike + The Mechanics, *The Living Years*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5hr64MxYpgk) is about speaking across generations; [Delaney & Bonnie, *Where There's a Will, There's a Way*](https://www.allmusic.com/song/where-theres-a-will-theres-a-way-live-at-fairfield-hall-12-07-1969--mt0054386944) is a neat title pun, not a legal guide. [BBC Money Box](https://www.bbc.co.uk/sounds/play/m002wj1f) has also discussed the lack of wills. None of these require playing copyrighted songs on the Aletheia site.

## The four-question family conversation

**Who?** Who inherits, and who is the replacement? **Where?** Where is the original will and the provider list? **What next?** Who knows how to notify companies and manage household bills? **What lasts?** Would outright gifts, regular family saving, or a modest pooled fund best support later generations?

A family legacy is not necessarily an enormous trust deed. Sometimes it's a well-written will, a tidy folder, a children's savings account and relatives who actually talk to one another.
